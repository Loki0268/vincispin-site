How the peg pyramid works

The board forms a triangle of staggered pegs, with each row one peg wider than the last. A ball released from the top has an even chance of moving left or right at each peg, and that same split repeats all the way down. After crossing the rows, it settles into one of the bottom pockets — always one more pocket than the number of rows.

Because each path is made up of repeated fifty-fifty choices, most balls end up near the centre and only a few reach the edges. Mathematically, the layout is a Galton board, and the results follow a binomial curve. The prize table simply flips that curve around: common centre pockets pay less, while the rare outer pockets pay more.

Multipliers, odds, and the house margin

Each pocket value is broadly tied to its chance of landing, then trimmed by a small house margin. If you multiply the payout by the probability of reaching it, the total comes in slightly below one; that difference is the operator's edge. On most versions, the return to player sits somewhere around 97% to 99%, depending on the studio and the chosen settings.

A quick reality check: no row count or risk level turns the maths in your favour over time. The controls change variance — the size of the swings — not the long-term return.

Trusted releases use a certified random number generator for every deflection, and provably fair versions publish seed hashes so each result can be checked later. What none of them can do is let you guide the ball where you want it to go.

Six words worth knowing

The slot at the bottom of the board that holds the multiplier for the finished ball.
Rows
The number of peg lines the ball crosses; more rows mean more pockets and tighter odds.
Risk level
A setting that stretches or compresses the payout table without changing the core return much.
House margin
The small difference between the true odds and the payout that supports the operator.
Provably fair
A method that uses published hashes so a result can be checked after the round.
Variance
The size of the ups and downs around the average result — the thing the risk setting controls.

Straight answers to five questions

Is every drop genuinely random?

On licensed platforms, yes. Each deflection comes from an audited random number generator, and provably fair versions let you confirm after the round that the result was set before the ball moved.

Do more rows make the game pay better?

No. More rows add pockets and stretch the distribution, so the edge prizes get larger but less likely. The overall return stays broadly the same.

Which risk level is the smart choice?

There is no mathematically better option. Low risk smooths the ride, while high risk concentrates value in the outer pockets. Pick the one that suits your budget and temperament.

Does timing or button rhythm influence where a ball lands?

No. The path is set by the random generator, not by how quickly you press the button. Any pattern you think you see is just coincidence.
